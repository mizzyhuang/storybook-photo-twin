# European Picture-Book Photo Twin

Create one composite image: preserve the supplied photograph realistically unchanged and add a separate hand-drawn illustrated counterpart of its main person beside them. The illustration should feel like a contemporary European children's picture book, not an anime character, 3D mascot, vector icon, or photorealistic edit.

## Subject and placement

- Treat the most prominent intended person as the source character. If several people could reasonably be the subject, ask which one to illustrate.
- Preserve the photo's people, background, lighting, and crop. Do not redraw, beautify, replace, or cartoonize them.
- Put the illustrated twin in an open adjacent area, usually low in the frame, without obscuring the source person's face, key outfit, or a meaningful photo detail.
- Ground the character at the same apparent floor or step level. Keep it deliberately flat and two-dimensional; do not add a realistic cast shadow or photo-matched rendering.
- Use an independent pose informed by the source pose. It may lean, walk, hold an analogous object, or interact with an included pet, but should remain recognizably the same person.

## Illustration language

- Draw a large-headed, compact-bodied character with gently uneven proportions and a friendly, observational children's-book feel. Avoid chibi extremes.
- Use warm off-white or peach skin, rounded facial planes, a small nose, a tiny curved smile, and sparse facial features. Glasses, sunglasses, freckles, blush, and hairstyle are high-value identity cues.
- Use a quick, relaxed pencil or soft-ink contour whose weight and curvature vary naturally. Do not trace every silhouette as a uniform closed shape: allow short gaps, slightly misaligned joins, an occasional overshoot, and color fills that do not perfectly meet the line.
- Fill with muted, lightly warm, mostly flat color blocks. Paper grain, if present, should be barely perceptible and never describe fabric, material, or lighting. Avoid glossy gradients, airbrushed shading, digitally sterile flat vectors, and dense crayon texture.
- Draw hair as 4–8 directional, irregular grouped locks or curved strands, with a few loose ends escaping the silhouette. Do not use one smooth, heavy helmet-shaped hair mass.
- Prefer an asymmetrical, casually observed pose over bilateral balance. Let shoulder height, hand position, pant-leg width, shoe direction, and garment hems differ slightly where the pose permits.
- Add only the few internal marks needed for recognition: a button row, one zipper, a simple stripe/check pattern, one bag closure, or distinctive pet markings. Omit wrinkles, denim wash, leather shine, stitch runs, elaborate hardware, and decorative micro-detail.

## Outfit translation

- Match visible hairstyle, headwear, eyewear, palette, garment layering, silhouette, and footwear before adding decorative detail.
- Recreate distinctive accessories with simplified drawn equivalents: bags, jewelry, scarves, drinks, phones, umbrellas, and pets.
- Preserve visual hierarchy: recognizable color blocking and silhouette first, then one or two iconic accessories. Do not invent small logos or illegible text.

## Quality bar

The result should read as a real-photo fashion or travel snapshot with a charming illustrated wardrobe double. The character must be visibly derived from the person in the photo while retaining a handmade, airy, flat picture-book quality.

Do not alter the original photo; convert the whole scene to illustration; make a generic avatar unrelated to the outfit; use manga/anime conventions; use 3D, clay, glossy-sticker, or hyper-clean corporate-vector aesthetics; add heavy shadows, frames, captions, or watermarks unless requested.
